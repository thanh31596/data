Part 1 of 4

Search Engine Optimisation

LO1 — Investigate how SEO adds value to a company's marketing strategy. Goal: help the right people find BrewLab on Google before they find a competitor.
1.1

1.1 What is SEO, in plain terms?

Definition
Search Engine Optimisation (SEO) is the work of making your website as visible as possible on a search platform, using unpaid keywords and phrases tied to your content, so that more of the right people click through to your site.

The three ideas inside that sentence

  • Visible — appear high on the results page, not on page five.
  • Unpaid — this is earned ranking, not advertising you buy.
  • Right people — traffic that actually wants what you sell.
BrewLab lens
When someone in Melbourne types "specialty coffee beans delivered", BrewLab wants to be the first result they trust — without paying for every click.
1.2

1.2 Why SEO matters — where people search now

Most searching happens on a phone. If BrewLab's site is slow or awkward on mobile, good ranking is wasted.

  • Mobile phones have overtaken desktops for internet searching.
  • 95.9% of users access the internet via mobile
  • 61.4% via laptops and desktops

Source: DataReportal / GWI, Global Overview, April 2025.

1.3

1.3 Why SEO matters — which search engine to optimise for

One engine dominates. For BrewLab in Australia, "SEO" mostly means "rank well on Google".

  • Google's worldwide share of search is about 89.7%.
  • In Australia it is even higher, about 92.2%.
  • Optimise for Google first; other engines follow similar rules.

Source: Statcounter GlobalStats, March 2025.

1.4

1.4 Two halves of SEO: on-page and off-page

Owned media
The content on BrewLab's own website — plus the keywords and key phrases attached to it. This is what BrewLab fully controls.

On-page SEO — improving items on your own site: page titles, headers, image labels, the words on the page, and the underlying code.

Off-page SEO — improving external signals: links from other sites pointing to you, and your social media presence.

Both aim at one thing
A higher position in the search rankings.
1.5

1.5 Keyword research — the words your audience actually types

Keyword research means working out the words a target customer would type into Google, then focusing on the ones that are both relevant and widely used.

  • Relevant but rarely searched → few visitors.
  • Popular but irrelevant → visitors who bounce.
  • The sweet spot is relevant and popular.

Free tools such as Keyword Tool (keywordtool.io) suggest real phrases people search.

BrewLab keyword map

A customer might type any of these:

specialty coffee Melbourne best coffee beans online AU single origin espresso beans coffee subscription delivered how to brew pour over

The last one is a question — a bridge to content strategy on the next slide.

1.6

1.6 Content strategy — matching content to search intent

Ranking for a word is not enough. The page has to give searchers what they actually wanted.

  • Match search intent — ask "what does this searcher want to find?" and build the page around that answer.
  • Answer frequent questions — turn common questions into clear pages.
BrewLab example
Someone searching "how to brew pour over" wants a guide, not a product list. So BrewLab writes a short pour-over guide — which then earns the click and introduces its beans.
Intent → the page that satisfies it.
1.7

1.7 How search engines find you: Crawl → Index → Rank

Every search engine runs the same three steps. Knowing them tells BrewLab where to intervene.

Where BrewLab acts
Crawl: make pages easy to reach through links and clean structure.  Index: use clear titles so pages are stored correctly.  Rank: match keywords and intent so BrewLab is a "best match".
1.8

Five common checks to push a page higher, and one free tool for each. BrewLab does not need all at once — start where the weakness is.

A/B Testing

LO2 — Apply A/B testing to compare digital marketing effectiveness. Goal: stop guessing which email or page works, and let visitors decide.
2.1

2.1 What is A/B testing?

Definition
A/B testing (also called split testing or bucket testing) compares two versions of a digital asset — a web page, email, or social post — to see which one prompts more action from viewers.
  • Version A is usually what you have now.
  • Version B changes something you think could do better.
  • The winner is the one that gets more of the action you care about.
BrewLab lens
Does the button "Shop now" or "Get 15% off" get more clicks? A/B testing answers it with evidence, not opinion.
2.2

2.2 How A/B testing works

  1. Show the two versions at random to visitors or email subscribers — roughly half see A, half see B.
  2. Measure a clear number, such as click-through rate or number of inquiries.
  3. After enough responses, keep the version that wins.
The number you compare
$$\text{conversion rate}=\frac{\text{people who acted}}{\text{people who saw it}}$$

Random splitting matters: it keeps the two audiences comparable, so the version — not the audience — explains the difference.

Keep Version A — it converts more visitors.
2.3

2.3 Where you can use it, and how far it scales

Any stage of the funnel

A/B testing can lift any step — a headline that wins more Attention, or a checkout button that wins more Action. Test wherever people drop off.

BrewLab
Test the subject line to lift email opens; test the product photo to lift add-to-cart.

Simple vs A/B/n

  • A/B (simplest): two versions of one element.
  • A/B/n: more than two versions of a single element tested together — A, B, C, and so on.

More versions need more traffic to reach a trustworthy result.

2.4

2.4 Case study: how Netflix tests artwork

Netflix shows different thumbnail images for the same title and measures which draws more viewers. Here are two images for one film, scored by two viewer groups.

Viewer profileScore, Image AScore, Image B
Comedy fans5.76.3
Romance fans7.26.5

Image A: the couple, close together. Image B: the lead actor alone, smiling.

Read the result
Comedy fans preferred Image B. Romance fans preferred Image A. There is no single "best" image — the winner depends on the viewer.
The lesson for BrewLab
The best creative can differ by audience segment. Rather than one email for everyone, BrewLab can show espresso lovers one hero image and filter-coffee lovers another — the same idea Netflix uses at scale.
2.Q

Knowledge check — A/B testing

Q1. BrewLab sends Version A to its 8am subscribers and Version B to its 8pm subscribers, then declares B the winner. What is the flaw?
A/B testing needs a random split so the audiences are comparable. Splitting by send time introduces a second difference, so you cannot tell what caused the change.
Q2. The Netflix case shows that comedy fans and romance fans preferred different images. The strongest takeaway is:
Each segment favoured a different image, so there is no single winner for everyone. That is the case for tailoring creative to segments.

Social Media & GenAI in your strategy

LO3 — Evaluate the use of social media and GenAI as part of one's marketing strategy. Goal: grow BrewLab's reach and engagement without creating new risks.
3.1

3.1 Why social media belongs in the strategy

Social media is another channel to reach customers — and, done well, it sends them back to your website.

It gives BrewLab a channel to:

  • Promote brand awareness — more people learn BrewLab exists.
  • Build reputation — show quality and personality over time.
  • Engage with customers — and drive that traffic to the website, feeding sales.
Engagement channelled back to the site.
3.2

3.2 A social media strategy in five moves

A repeatable loop: set goals, create, get discovered, respond, then measure and improve.

BrewLab in practice
Aim: 20% more site visits from Instagram this quarter (a KPI). Post brewing tips using #MelbourneCoffee, reply to every comment, then check which posts sent the most traffic — and make more of those.
3.3

3.3 GenAI in marketing — the upside and the data risk

Where it helps

  • Draft captions, product descriptions, and email variants fast.
  • Generate first-draft images and campaign ideas to react to.
  • Summarise customer comments to find common themes.
BrewLab use
Generate ten caption options for a new roast, then A/B test the two strongest.

Where the risk is

  • Your data: pasting confidential sales figures or customer lists into a public tool can expose them.
  • Competitor benefit: prompts and data you share may inform models others also use.
  • Accuracy: outputs can be wrong or off-brand, so a human must review before publishing.
Rule of thumb
Use GenAI to draft and speed up — not as the final word, and not with data you would not email to a stranger.
3.Q

Knowledge check — social media & GenAI

Q1. In BrewLab's five-move social strategy, "20% more site visits from Instagram this quarter" is an example of which move?
It is a specific, measurable target — a KPI. It sets what success looks like before any content is made.
Q2. A BrewLab staffer pastes the full customer email list into a public GenAI tool to "write a nicer newsletter". The main concern is:
Sharing private data with a public tool risks exposing it. The task itself is fine — the risk is feeding confidential data into a system you do not control.

Crisis Mitigation on Social Media

LO4 — Explore aspects of crisis mitigation. Goal: when a bad review spreads, protect BrewLab's reputation and turn the situation around.
4.1

4.1 The scenario BrewLab now faces

What happened
An incident with one order was aired on social media. It sparked a wave of negative comments and reviews about the BrewLab brand.

The question for the room: how would you turn the situation around?

Silence tends to make it worse. A calm, structured response can rebuild trust. The next slide gives a five-step approach.

One incident, many reactions.
4.2

4.2 Five steps to steady the brand

BrewLab in action
Monitor mentions daily; find that one delivery batch was late; reply publicly with a genuine apology; point to BrewLab's track record; then offer the customer a direct fix by phone or email.
The aim
Move the conversation from a public pile-on to a private, solvable problem — while everyone watching sees a brand that responds well.
